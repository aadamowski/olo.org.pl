One's and two's complement: signed integers

Table of Contents

Binary arithmetic — how machines subtract by adding

A computer’s adder only knows how to add. Subtraction, negative numbers, and the sign bit itself all have to be built out of that one operation — and the trick that makes it possible is the complement: for every number, a partner that completes it to a round total. Flip every bit and you get the one’s complement; flip and add one and you get the two’s complement, and with it A−B becomes A+(−B), an addition. This page builds the idea from the definition up: what a complement is with respect to a number, the three historical ways to write signed values, negation, addition, subtraction by addition, the end-around carry that one’s complement arithmetic needs, signed overflow, and the exact range each system buys you.

The one-sentence idea. The complement of x with respect to a fixed total T is what x needs added to it to reach T: it is T−x. Choose T=2n in n bits and that partner is the negation — so subtraction collapses into addition, and one adder circuit does everything.

1 — What a complement is, with respect to a number

“Complement” is a relative word: a number never has a complement in the abstract, only a complement with respect to some fixed reference total. The complement of x with respect to T is the amount that must be added to x to reach exactly T, equivalently, T−x. Two gears meshing, two angles making a right angle, two quantities making a round sum: in every case the complement is defined by the total it completes.

The reference totals that matter are the roundest ones in each base. In two-digit decimal the candidates are T=99 (all nines, one less than the modulus) and T=100 (the modulus itself, 102). They give the 9’s complement and the 10’s complement. The 9’s version has a gift: since 99−x subtracts each digit of x from 9 independently, it needs no borrows — each digit is simply flipped to its partner under 9 (0↔9, 1↔8, 2↔7, 3↔6, 4↔5). In binary the same two choices reappear, and the digit-flip gift becomes a bit-flip.

Complement with respect to TCT(x)=T−xx+CT(x)=TThe defining property is the second line: a number plus its complement makes the reference total, exactly.
Decimal, k digits10k complement (radix): C10k(x)=10k−x10k−1 complement (diminished radix): C10k−1(x)=(10k−1)−xThe diminished-radix version is digitwise: each digit d maps to 9−d, no borrows. And the two are one apart: radix complement = diminished complement + 1.

Then the two’s complement is one step further:C2n(x)=x+1=(2n−1−x)+1=2n−x
Why the modulus total gives negationKeep all terms, then reduce. Working modulo 2n, which is exactly what an n-bit register does, since a carry out of the top bit falls off the end:−x≡−x+2n−2n≡(2n−x)−2n≡2n−x(mod2n)So inside a register, the pattern 2n−x behaves in every addition exactly as −x does. The two’s complement is not an encoding trick layered on top of arithmetic — it is negation, in the only arithmetic the hardware has.

Complements turn subtraction into addition — already in decimal

  • Compute 52−37 with the 10’s complement: 52+63=115, drop the carried 1 (the modulus leaving the register), and the answer is 15.
  • With the 9’s complement: 52+62=114. Nothing can be dropped — instead the carried 1 is added back at the bottom: 14+1=15. That wrap-around has a name you will meet again in Step 6: the end-around carry.
  • Mechanical calculators and early computers genuinely subtracted this way: one adder, no subtractor, no borrow chains.

2 — Sign-magnitude vs one’s vs two’s complement

An n-bit register holds 2n patterns. Unsigned arithmetic spends them all on 0 to 2n−1. To also write negative numbers, three systems compete — all three agree on the positives (sign bit 0, plain binary magnitude) and differ only in how a pattern with sign bit 1 is read.

Sign-magnitudeThe top bit is a pure sign flag; the remaining n−1 bits hold |x|. Reading is effortless — this is how humans write −5, but the adder must compare signs and magnitudes before it can even start, and 0 gets two patterns: 0000 and 1000 (+0 and −0).
One’s complementA negative is the bitwise flip of its positive: pattern value =−x~ read as “flip to negate”. Negation is free and symmetric, but 0 still has two patterns (0000 and 1111), and addition needs the end-around carry correction of Step 6.
Two’s complementA negative is the flip plus one: pattern p with top bit 1 has value p−2n. Equivalently, read the bits as a weighted sum where the top bit weighs −2n−1 instead of +2n−1. One pattern for zero, one plain unsigned adder for everything — and the spend is asymmetric: one extra negative, −2n−1, with no positive partner.

Why two’s complement won. Sign-magnitude survives where humans read the bits (the sign bit of IEEE floating point) and one’s complement survives inside the Internet checksum (Step 6), but general-purpose integer arithmetic is two’s complement everywhere: a single zero, positives and negatives through one adder, and subtraction with no special cases. The price — one un-negatable value at the bottom of the range — is Step 3’s edge case and Step 8’s asymmetry.

3 — Negation: each system flips something different

Negation is where the three systems show their personalities most plainly. Sign-magnitude flips one bit — the sign — and touches nothing else. One’s complement flips every bit: negation is the complement operation, which is exactly the diminished-radix complement of Step 1. Two’s complement flips every bit and adds one, completing the move from the diminished total 2n−1 to the full modulus 2n.

Edge cases, one per system

  • Sign-magnitude and one’s complement: negating 0 produces the other zero: 10000000 or 11111111. Harmless, but code that compares patterns bit-for-bit sees two different zeros.
  • Two’s complement: negating −128 (pattern 10000000) flips to 01111111 and adds 1, landing back on 10000000, itself. There is no +128 in 8 bits to land on. In C this is why abs(INT_MIN) stays negative; the operation silently overflows (Step 7).
  • Try it above: set the two’s complement slider to −128 and watch the output pattern equal the input.

4 — Addition: one unsigned adder serves every sign

Here is the payoff of the two’s complement definition. Because a negative pattern is its value modulo 2n, adding two patterns bit by bit — carries and all, with any carry out of the top bit simply discarded — produces the pattern of the true sum, whenever that sum fits the range. No sign comparison, no magnitude subtraction, no cases: the same circuit that adds unsigned numbers adds signed ones, and the result is even correct read as unsigned when both inputs are. (One’s complement almost manages this: its additions are correct up to the end-around carry repair of Step 6. Sign-magnitude addition, by contrast, is a small decision tree of its own.)

5 — Subtraction by addition: negate, then add

With negation reduced to a complement, subtraction needs no new hardware. The identity, with every term kept until the modulus absorbs it:

The subtraction identityA−B=A−B+2n−2n=A+(2n−B)−2n≡A+(2n−B)(mod2n)Adding and subtracting 2n changes nothing; regrouping exposes 2n−B, the two’s complement of B; and the trailing −2n is precisely the carry-out the register discards. In one’s complement the same identity holds with 2n−1 in place of 2n, and the discarded amount comes back as the end-around carry, which is why that system needs Step 6.

Reading the carry-out in a subtraction

  • Two’s complement: the carry-out is pure exhaust — the 2n from the identity leaving the register. Discard it unconditionally. (Some architectures keep it as a “no borrow” flag, which is bookkeeping, not arithmetic.)
  • One’s complement: a carry-out of 1 means the raw sum is one short — the missing 2n−1 bookkeeping — and it must be added back at the least significant bit. A carry-out of 0 means the raw pattern is already a finished (negative) one’s complement result: flip it to read its magnitude.
  • Try A=9, B=25 in both systems: two’s complement shows carry-out 0 and pattern 11110000 (−16 directly); one’s shows carry-out 0 and 11101111, which flips to magnitude 16.

6 — End-around carry: the carry that comes back

Why does one’s complement addition need a repair that two’s complement addition doesn’t? The diminished modulus is the reason. Adding two one’s complement patterns works modulo 2n−1, not 2n, but the register discards multiples of 2n. Each carry out of the top bit therefore throws away 2n when it should only have thrown away 2n−1: the sum comes out exactly 1 too small. The fix is to take that carry and add it back in at the least significant bit — the carry ends around. Two’s complement, whose modulus is the register’s own 2n, discards exactly the right amount and needs no repair. Step through all four behaviours:

Where end-around carry still earns its keep. The Internet checksum (used by IPv4, TCP and UDP) sums a message in 16-bit words using one’s complement arithmetic — end-around carry included — and stores the complement of the sum. Why keep the older system? Endianness independence: the checksum comes out byte-swapped-identical on big- and little-endian machines, and a correctly checksummed message sums to all ones, which any receiver can verify with the same adder. Two’s complement arithmetic could not offer either property as cheaply.

7 — Overflow: the sign bit lies

Discarding the carry-out is safe only while the true result stays inside [−2n−1,2n−1−1]. Step outside it and the pattern still forms — addition never fails — but it now encodes a value of the wrong sign: 100+50 in 8 bits produces 10010110, which reads as −106. That event is signed overflow, and it is not the carry-out: 100+(−50) also produces a carry-out of 1, yet its answer 50 is exactly right. Overflow has a precise test, in two equivalent forms:

Sign ruleOverflow happens exactly when the two addends have the same sign and the result’s sign differs from theirs. Adding numbers of opposite signs can never overflow: the true sum lies between them, hence inside the range. The same rule covers subtraction, reading A−B as A+(−B): overflow needs A and B of opposite signs and a result whose sign differs from A’s.
Carry rule (what hardware tests)Let cin be the carry into the sign bit and cout the carry out of it. ThenV=cin⊕coutOverflow (V=1) exactly when the two carries differ — the sign column’s arithmetic disagrees with its neighbours about which side of the boundary the sum fell on. This XOR is the overflow flag in every mainstream CPU.

Overflow vs carry-out — keep them separate

  • Carry-out = 1 means the unsigned sum exceeded 2n−1. It fires constantly in perfectly correct signed arithmetic — every time a negative addend is involved, roughly.
  • Overflow = 1 means the signed sum left the representable range and the stored pattern has the wrong sign. This is the one signed code must check (or rule out by widening).
  • Neither flag stops the addition. The pattern is always produced; the flags are the only record that something needs attention.

8 — Range: counting the patterns

Every system spends the same budget, 2n patterns — and the range table is just the accounting. Sign-magnitude and one’s complement split the budget evenly between negative and positive but waste one pattern on a second zero, so both stop at ±(2n−1−1). Two’s complement spends the would-be second zero on one more negative value, reaching −2n−1: the range is asymmetric by exactly one, the same asymmetry that made −128 un-negatable in Step 3. The arc, in one breath. A complement is always relative: the complement of x with respect to a total T is T−x. With respect to 2n−1 that partner is a mere bit-flip away but leaves arithmetic one short, repaid by the end-around carry; with respect to 2n it is exactly negation in register arithmetic, so a single adder adds, subtracts and negates — and the only remaining hazards are the finite range: overflow when a sum’s sign flips, and the lone extra negative at the bottom that has nothing to flip to.
