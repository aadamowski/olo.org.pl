Hilbert Space and the Hypercomplex Tower: ℝ, ℂ, ℍ, 𝕆, 𝕊 and Beyond

Table of Contents

Interactive explainer — Inner products, completeness, and the Cayley–Dickson ladder

Two great constructions run through modern mathematics. One goes up in dimension without end: Hilbert space, where vectors can be whole functions and sums can have infinitely many terms. The other doubles dimension in discrete jumps — ℝ → ℂ → ℍ → 𝕆 → 𝕊 → the 32-dimensional trigintaduonions — and pays a toll at every jump, surrendering one cherished law of arithmetic each time. This page builds both, step by step, and shows exactly where they meet.

1 — Hilbert space: geometry with infinitely many directions

A Hilbert space is a vector space equipped with an inner product — a machine that takes two vectors and returns a number measuring how much they point the same way — and one extra demand: the space must be complete, meaning every sequence of vectors that ought to converge to something actually converges to a vector inside the space. Inner product gives geometry (lengths, angles, projections); completeness guarantees the geometry has no holes.

Everything starts with the inner product. In the plane, for u=(u1,u2) and v=(v1,v2), it is the familiar dot product. The definition, and the three quantities it buys you, are collected in one table:

QuantityFormulaWhat it means
Inner product⟨u,v⟩=u1v1+u2v2A single number: positive when u and v lean together, zero when perpendicular, negative when opposed.
Length (norm)‖u‖=⟨u,u⟩=u12+u22Pythagoras, restated: a vector’s length is its inner product with itself, square-rooted.
Anglecos⁡θ=⟨u,v⟩‖u‖‖v‖The inner product, normalised by the two lengths, is exactly the cosine of the angle between the vectors.
Projectionprojuv=⟨u,v⟩⟨u,u⟩·uThe shadow of v along u — the part of v that u can “see”. What remains, v−projuv, is perpendicular to u.

Completeness: no holes in the space

Geometry alone does not make a Hilbert space. Consider the rational numbers ℚ on a line: you can add them and measure distances, yet the sequence 1, 1.4, 1.41, 1.414, … — rationals closing in on 2 — is a Cauchy sequence (its terms get arbitrarily close to each other) whose limit is missing from ℚ. A space with such a hole is an inner product space but not a Hilbert space. Completeness is the demand that every Cauchy sequence converges to a point of the space itself.

The two flagship infinite-dimensional examples

SpaceIts “vectors” and inner productWhy it is complete / useful
ℓ2— square-summable sequencesx=(x1,x2,x3,…) with ∑|xi|2<∞, and ⟨x,y⟩=∑xiy¯iThe direct infinite generalisation of ℂn. The condition ∑|xi|2<∞ is exactly “finite length”, and the inner-product series always converges by Cauchy–Schwarz.
L2— square-integrable functionsf a function on [a,b] with ∫|f|2<∞, and ⟨f,g⟩=∫abf(x)g(x)dxFunctions become points in a space; the integral is their dot product. Quantum mechanics lives here: a wavefunction ψ is a unit vector, and ⟨φ,ψ⟩ is a probability amplitude.

In L2, the trigonometric functions 1,cos⁡x,sin⁡x,cos⁡2x,sin⁡2x,… form an orthonormal basis — infinitely many mutually perpendicular unit directions. Any square-integrable function is a (possibly infinite) combination of them: its Fourier series. Completeness is precisely the theorem that the partial sums converge to the function in the L² sense. 2 — The hypercomplex tower at a glance

A hypercomplex number system is what you get when you try to keep doing arithmetic — add, subtract, multiply, take lengths — in more dimensions than the familiar number line and plane. There is a classical construction that doubles the dimension at every step (Section 3), producing an infinite ladder. But arithmetic gets worse as you climb. The whole story of the tower is in this ledger of what survives:

“Alternative” is the weakened associativity law a·(a·b)=(a·a)·b and (a·b)·b=a·(b·b) — associativity when two of the three factors coincide. It is strong enough to keep octonion division working. Below the sedenions nothing in the table is left except addition, a conjugation, and a notion of length that no longer respects multiplication.

3 — The Cayley–Dickson construction: how each level is built from the last

The doubling machine is due to Cayley and Dickson. Its input is an algebra A with a conjugation (an operation a↦a* that flips the sign of every imaginary part, like complex conjugation). Its output is a new algebra A2 whose elements are ordered pairs (a, b) of elements of A — so the dimension exactly doubles. Three definitions do all the work. They are stated once, in full generality, and then every algebra in the tower is just this recipe applied repeatedly:

IngredientDefinition in A2Notes
Elements(a,b),a,b∈AThink “a is the old part, b is the coefficient of one brand-new imaginary unit ℓ”. Formally (a,b) means a+bℓ.
Conjugation(a,b)=(a,−b)Conjugate the old part; negate the new part. For ℝ, a=a, so ℂ’s rule (a,b)=(a,−b) is ordinary complex conjugation.
Multiplication(a,b)(c,d)=(ac−db,da+bc)Every symbol on the right is an operation inside A. This one line, applied recursively, generates the multiplication tables of ℂ, ℍ, 𝕆, 𝕊, 𝕋, …
The new unitℓ=(0,1)Check it from the multiplication rule: (0,1)(0,1)=(0·0−1·1,1·0+1·0)=(−1,0). Each doubling manufactures a fresh square root of −1, perpendicular to all previous ones.
ℓ2=(−1,0)=−1

Worked check: the recipe really gives ℂ from ℝ

Take A = ℝ, where conjugation does nothing (a* = a). Apply the multiplication rule with all terms kept visible, in three steps — no step silently drops or rearranges anything:

StepComputationWhat happened
1 — Substitute(a,b)(c,d)=(ac−db,da+bc)The general Cayley–Dickson rule, with the pair components named.
2 — Use ℝ’s conjugation=(ac−db,da+bc) since d=dand c=c in ℝOnly the two conjugates are replaced; every product term is still present, in the same order.
3 — Reorder real products=(ac−bd,ad+bc)Real multiplication commutes, so db=bd and da=ad. This is exactly the school rule for (a+bi)(c+di). The familiar formula was the doubling rule all along.

Run the same recipe on ℂ and the output is ℍ: writing a quaternion as a pair of complex numbers, q=(a,b) with a,b∈ℂ, is the standard “complex pair” form q=a+bj. Run it on ℍ to get 𝕆, on 𝕆 to get 𝕊, on 𝕊 to get the trigintaduonions 𝕋. One caveat explains the falling laws of Figure 5: the recipe’s output inherits a law only if the input had a stronger one. Doubling a commutative algebra yields a merely associative one waiting to happen, and so on — each application spends one law to buy twice the dimensions.

4 — Walking the tower, one algebra at a time

ℝ — the real numbers (dimension 1)

The ground floor is a Hilbert space already: ℝ is ℝ1 with inner product ⟨a,b⟩=ab. It is the only level with a total ordering compatible with arithmetic (a<b implies a+c<b+c, and products of positives are positive). Everything above ℝ¹ will have to give that up — there is no way to say whether i is “positive” that survives multiplication, since i2=−1 would then have to be positive too.

ℂ — the complex numbers (dimension 2): pay ordering, gain roots

ℂ=ℝ2 as a Hilbert space, with the extra gift of multiplication. The imaginary unit i=(0,1) is the first Cayley–Dickson ℓ. Multiplying by i rotates the plane by 90° counter-clockwise, which is why i2=−1 is forced geometrically: two quarter-turns make a half-turn, and a half-turn sends every number to its negative.

ℍ — the quaternions (dimension 4): pay commutativity, gain 3D rotation

Hamilton’s quaternions (1843) write q=a+bi+cj+dk with three imaginary units obeying the relations he carved into Brougham Bridge: i2=j2=k2=ijk=−1. From those, the full multiplication of units follows — and it is ordered: ij=k but ji=−k. Rotations in 3D do not commute either (tip a book forward-then-yaw versus yaw-then-forward), and that is precisely why quaternions, not despite but because of non-commutativity, are the working language of 3D rotation in graphics, robotics and spacecraft attitude control.

𝕆 — the octonions (dimension 8): pay associativity, keep division

An octonion has seven imaginary units e1…e7 (with e0=1), each squaring to −1. Their multiplication is encoded by the Fano plane: seven points, seven lines of three points each. The rule: the three units on any line form a quaternion-style triple — the product of any two of them is the third, with the sign fixed by the line’s cyclic direction (click a line in Figure 10). But with three different lines involved, brackets start to matter. Here is a complete, checkable witness, with every intermediate product evaluated:

StepComputationWhat happened
1 — Left bracketing, inside first(e1·e2)·e4=e3·e4e1·e2=e3, because {1, 2, 3} is a Fano line with cyclic order 1 → 2 → 3.
2 — Left bracketing, finish(e1·e2)·e4=e7e3·e4=e7, from the line {3, 4, 7} with cyclic order 3 → 4 → 7.
3 — Right bracketing, inside firste1·(e2·e4)=e1·e6e2·e4=e6, from the line {2, 4, 6} with cyclic order 2 → 4 → 6. Same three factors, different grouping.
4 — Right bracketing, finishe1·(e2·e4)=−e7e1·e6=−e7: on the line {1, 7, 6} the pair (1, 6) runs against the cyclic order 1 → 7 → 6, so the sign flips.
5 — Compare(e1e2)e4=+e7The two bracketings differ — not by a little, but by a sign on the whole answer. Associativity is dead.
e1(e2e4)=−e7

What survives is alternativity: bracketings still agree whenever two of the three factors are equal, and that is enough to keep inverses and the norm law working. 𝕆 is the last division algebra, and the last normed one — Hurwitz’s theorem says there is no ninth dimension where this could continue.

𝕊 — the sedenions (dimension 16): pay division itself

Double once more and the failure is no longer about the order of operations — it is about existence. The sedenions contain zero divisors: non-zero elements whose product is exactly zero. That kills division (if xy=0 with x≠0, then y has no inverse), kills the norm law (‖x‖·‖y‖>0 but ‖xy‖=0), and kills alternativity too. 𝕋 — the trigintaduonions (dimension 32): the construction outlives the arithmetic

Nothing stops the machine. One more doubling gives the trigintaduonions: 32-dimensional numbers x=∑xiei with e0=1 and thirty-one imaginary units, built as pairs of sedenions, (a,b) with a,b∈𝕊, multiplied by the same one-line rule. (One proposed naming scheme also calls this level the pathions, the 64-dimensional level the chingons, the 128-dimensional the routons and the 256-dimensional the voudons — playful names signalling that these are now objects of study, not of calculation.)

What is true up here:

FeatureSituation in the trigintaduonions (dim 32)
Still standingAddition, subtraction, real scalar multiplication; conjugation x↦x; the quadratic norm N(x)=x·x=∑xi2, a non-negative real; power-associativity — powers xn are unambiguous because the subalgebra generated by any single element is commutative and associative (a general Cayley–Dickson theorem, true at every level, forever).
Long goneCommutativity, associativity, alternativity, division, and the multiplicative norm law — all lost at or before the sedenions and never regained. The sedenion zero divisors of Figure 11 embed unchanged: the same (e3+e10)(e6−e15)=0 holds verbatim in 𝕋, since 𝕊 sits inside 𝕋 as the pairs (a,0).
What proliferatesZero divisors become abundant and structured — in 𝕊 and beyond they organise into interlocking families (in 𝕊, the zero divisors built from basis units form patterns related to the “box” of 84 distinguished pairs). At 32 dimensions and above, classifying them is a research topic, not a table.
Why anyone caresThe tower’s well-behaved floors explain deep facts elsewhere: 𝕆 underlies the exceptional Lie groups and appears in string-theory compactifications; the pattern of losses (Hurwitz, and Bott–Milnor’s result that only dimensions 1, 2, 4, 8 admit the relevant parallelisable spheres S0,S1,S3,S7) ties the tower to topology. The higher floors are studied for the zero-divisor geometry itself and for proposed applications in signal processing and physics — with the honest caveat that without division, “solving equations” up here is a different and harder game.

5 — Where the two constructions meet

The tower and Hilbert space were never separate stories. Equip any level of the tower with its standard inner product — for x=∑xiei and y=∑yiei, take ⟨x,y⟩=∑xiyi, equivalently ⟨x,y⟩=Re(x·y*) — and:

FactStatementConsequence
Every floor is a Hilbert space, as an inner product spaceℝ≅ℝ1Finite-dimensional inner product spaces are automatically complete, so each floor is a Hilbert space with extra structure (a multiplication) laid on top of the geometry.
ℂ≅ℝ2
ℍ≅ℝ4
𝕆≅ℝ8
𝕊≅ℝ16
𝕋≅ℝ32
The norm is the Hilbert norm‖x‖2=⟨x,x⟩=x·x*=∑xi2The “length” that Hurwitz’s theorem is about is exactly the Hilbert-space length. Normed division algebra = Hilbert space whose multiplication never stretches lengths unexpectedly.
The tower stops cooperating, Hilbert space doesn’tdim can be any n, or ∞, for a Hilbert space — but normed division only for n∈{{1,2,4,8}}Hilbert space asks only for linear structure + inner product + completeness, all of which scale to any dimension. The tower additionally asks for a well-behaved multiplication, and that is the scarce resource.
ℂ graduatesQuantum theory = Hilbert space over ℂThe second floor of the tower supplies the scalars for the infinite-dimensional theory: states are unit vectors in a complex Hilbert space, observables are its self-adjoint operators, and ⟨φ,ψ⟩ is the transition amplitude. The two constructions are, in the end, one subject.
The one-paragraph summary

Start with ℝ. Ask for more directions while keeping arithmetic perfect, and Cayley–Dickson doubling gives you ℂ, ℍ, 𝕆 — charging ordering, then commutativity, then associativity — after which Hurwitz’s theorem proves the shop is closed: 𝕊 and the trigintaduonions beyond it keep the construction but lose division to zero divisors. Ask instead for more directions while keeping only geometry — inner product, length, angle, projection — plus the completeness that makes limits land, and you get Hilbert space, which happily extends to infinitely many dimensions, houses Fourier series and quantum states, and contains every floor of the tower as a finite-dimensional special case.
