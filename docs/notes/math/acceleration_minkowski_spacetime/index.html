Acceleration and Time Dilation in Minkowski Spacetime

Table of Contents

Special relativity · Minkowski geometry

A moving clock ticks slowly — that part depends only on its speed. So where does acceleration come in? It comes in by bending the clock’s path through spacetime. Once you draw motion the way Minkowski taught us to draw it, acceleration stops being a footnote to time dilation and becomes the thing that decides which history a clock lives through — and therefore how much time it actually experiences.

The one-sentence version: time dilation at any instant is set by velocity alone, γ = 1/√(1 − v²/c²). Acceleration never appears in that formula — but acceleration is what changes v, curves the worldline, and makes the total elapsed proper time depend on the whole journey. That is why the accelerating twin comes home younger.

Step 1The interval — all dimensions
Δs² = −c²Δt² + Δx² + Δy² + Δz²

The Minkowski interval between two events. Time enters with a minus sign; all three space dimensions — x, y and z — enter with plus signs.

Step 2Rearrange for proper time — still all dimensions
c²Δτ² = −Δs² = c²Δt² − Δx² − Δy² − Δz²

Proper time τ is defined by c²Δτ² = −Δs² for a timelike interval. Negating the Step 1 expression flips every sign: the time term becomes positive and each space term — x, y and z alike — becomes negative. Nothing has been dropped yet.

Step 3Now specialise to motion along x only
c²Δτ² = c²Δt² − Δx²

Only now do y and z leave the formula — and only because this page mostly studies motion along a single line, so Δy = 0 and Δz = 0. Their terms in Step 2 are then zero and drop out, leaving the one-dimensional form used in the diagrams below.

The Minkowski interval and its rearrangement into proper time τ, broken into steps: first the full interval, then the full rearrangement keeping y and z, then the one-dimensional special case. Everything on this page is these lines, drawn as pictures. Units below: c = 1 light-year per year, so light travels at 45°.

1 · The Minkowski map

Step 1 of 9

A Minkowski diagram throws away two space dimensions and promotes time to an axis. Horizontal position is x; vertical position is ct — time multiplied by the speed of light, so time and space share the same units (here, light-years). Any object’s history becomes a curve on this map: its worldline. A dot is an event — a place at a moment.

Figure 1 — The Minkowski map. Because one year of ct and one light-year of x are drawn the same length, anything moving at light speed travels at exactly 45°. Nothing massive can tilt further than that.

Three reading rules make every later diagram fall into place:

How to read it

  • Slope is speed. A vertical worldline is an object sitting still. The more a worldline leans away from vertical, the faster the object moves — lean is literally v/c.
  • The cone is a speed limit drawn as geometry. A worldline must always stay inside the future light cone of every event it passes through. Leaning to 45° would mean moving at c.
  • Clocks measure their own worldline. The time a traveller experiences — their proper time τ — is a property of the curve they trace, not of the map’s vertical axis. The next step makes that precise.

Keep one contrast in mind: on an ordinary road map, the straight line between two towns is the shortest route. In Minkowski’s geometry the sign in front of the time term flips that intuition — and the flip is the whole story of time dilation.

2 · Proper time is length

Step 2 of 9

For a short segment of worldline, the interval formula gives the proper time ticked by a clock riding that segment: c²dτ² = c²dt² − dx². Time contributes positively, space subtracts. So a clock that also moves through space accumulates less proper time than one that goes straight up in time. The calibration curves below — hyperbolas of constant τ — are the Minkowski equivalent of the circles a compass draws on an ordinary map: every point on one curve is the same proper time from the origin event.

How to read it

  • Each hyperbola marks events reachable with the same proper time τ from departure — τ = 1, 2, 3 years.
  • Moving sideways pushes you along the hyperbola, not outward: at 0.6c, reaching the τ = 2 curve takes 2.5 years of Earth time but still only 2 years on your clock.
  • These hyperbolas, not circles, are spacetime’s “distance contours” — a direct picture of the minus sign in the interval.

The reversed triangle inequality. Between two events, the straight (unaccelerated) worldline has the longest proper time of any route. Any detour through space — any leaning, bending worldline — totals less τ. “The shortest-looking path on paper is the longest in time.” Steps 4–6 are all consequences of this one fact.

Figure 2 — Calibration hyperbolas (ct)² − x² = (cτ)². Same τ, different Earth time: the moving clock’s event sits further up the page yet on the same hyperbola. That gap is time dilation, before any mention of acceleration.

3 · Velocity dilates

Step 3 of 9

Differentiate the interval and the proper-time rate falls out immediately: dτ = dt √(1 − v²/c²) = dt/γ. A clock moving at speed v through your frame ticks γ times slower — not because anything is wrong with it, but because part of its motion through spacetime is spent going sideways. On the diagram, the moving observer’s axes themselves tilt: their time axis leans by the angle whose tangent is v/c, and their space axis leans by the same angle — so their “now” slices the diagram diagonally. That symmetric tilt is the Lorentz transformation, as a picture.

Figure 3 — A moving frame at 0.60c (γ = 1.25). The traveller’s clock axis and simultaneity line tilt together — and both are drawn live from the Cruising speed slider below, the same slider that drives Figure 4. Disagreements about “now” — and about whose clock is slow — come straight from this tilt. No acceleration anywhere yet.

4 · Acceleration bends

Step 4 of 9

Velocity is the slope of a worldline. Acceleration is its rate of change of slope — the worldline bends. An unaccelerated clock draws a straight line and (Step 2) collects the maximum possible proper time between two events. An accelerated clock curves away and collects less. At each instant, the curved worldline is tangent to some straight worldline: the traveller momentarily is an inertial observer with that velocity, and that instant’s tilted frame from Figure 3. Acceleration is the continuous hand-off from one tilted frame to the next.

Figure 5 — Acceleration is bending. White ticks mark equal proper-time intervals along each worldline — 8 fit along the straight one, only 6 along the curved one. The curved worldline leans hard but stays inside the light cone everywhere: its steepest slope is 0.85c, never past the 45° cone. The curved traveller hasn’t been “slowed by a force” — they have simply taken a shorter route through time.

The precise statement

  • A clock’s instantaneous rate depends only on its instantaneous v — this is the clock hypothesis, tested to accelerations of ~1018 m/s² (Step 9).
  • But its total elapsed time is the accumulation along the whole curve — and acceleration is the only way to change the curve.
  • In the traveller’s own terms, what they feel is proper acceleration α = γ³a (for motion along the velocity): the reading on an accelerometer they carry. It is absolute — no frame choice makes a bent worldline straight.

Common misconception, corrected: “Acceleration causes time dilation, the way gravity does.” In special relativity there is no extra acceleration term to add. Two clocks with the same v(t) history tick identically even if one reached that history gently and the other violently. Acceleration matters indirectly but decisively: it authors the velocity history — and at a turnaround, it breaks the symmetry between two observers, as Step 6 shows.

5 · Adding up a journey

Step 5 of 9

Chop any worldline — however bent — into tiny straight pieces. Each piece is short enough that the velocity barely changes across it, so Step 3’s formula applies piece by piece: Δτ ≈ Δt √(1 − v²/c²). Add the pieces and let them shrink, and you get the master formula of this page. Acceleration is inside it implicitly: v(t) is whatever function the accelerations produced.

τ = ∫ √(1 − v(t)²/c²) dt  =  ∫ dt / γ(t) Proper time along any worldline. This single integral is the “Minkowski spacetime formula” for time dilation with acceleration included: feed it a velocity history — any history, however it was accelerated into being — and it returns exactly what the travelling clock reads.

Worked miniature — a clock whose speed profile over 4 Earth seconds is: accelerate 0 → 0.6c in the first second, cruise 2 seconds, decelerate back to rest in the last second. Applying the integral in three chunks:

Figure 6 — The integral, geometrically. Each straight segment contributes Δt/γ. Where the worldline leans more (higher v), equal vertical rises buy less proper time — the segments are “worth” less τ. The four straight segments meet at sharp corners — each corner a sudden jump in speed — and run at 0.03c, 0.32c, 0.62c and 0.93c; the smooth worldline’s steepest slope is likewise 0.93c. Everything stays well left of the 45° cone line, so nothing here reaches light speed.

6 · The twin paradox

Step 6 of 9

Each twin, coasting, sees the other’s clock run slow — so who is younger at reunion? The standoff is resolved by the worldlines. The stay-at-home twin follows one straight worldline: a single inertial frame the whole time. The traveller follows two different straight worldlines joined at the turnaround — and joining them requires acceleration. At the turnaround the traveller’s “now” line (Figure 3) swings violently: in the traveller’s accounting, a whole slab of Earth history jumps from “future” to “past” during that brief manoeuvre. That swing is where Earth’s clock “gains” the years. No swing, no reunion discrepancy; acceleration is what makes the two histories genuinely different rather than two descriptions of one history.

Figure 7 — Twins at 0.6c to a star 3 ly away. Earth: 5 yr out + 5 yr back = 10 yr. Traveller: each leg 5/γ = 4 yr of proper time, total 8 yr. The dashed lines are the traveller’s simultaneity slices just before and just after turning; Earth’s clock reading “jumps” by 3.6 yr (= 2vL/c²) between them — not because Earth time misbehaves, but because the traveller changed frames, and changing frames takes acceleration.

The same trip, three ways of seeing it

  • Geometry: the bent worldline is shorter in proper time than the straight one (Step 2’s reversed triangle inequality). Done — no paradox.
  • Frames: the traveller uses two inertial frames; the home twin uses one. The situations were never symmetric.
  • Feeling: the traveller feels the turnaround (accelerometer spikes); the home twin feels nothing. Proper acceleration is the physical marker of who took the detour.

Note what the integral (Step 5) says about the turnaround itself: making it gentler or sharper barely changes the total — the 2-year deficit is earned during the long fast legs. Acceleration’s role is qualitative (it enables the asymmetric journey) more than quantitative (its duration contributes little τ either way). Step 7 looks at the one case where acceleration is sustained the whole trip.

7 · Constant acceleration

Step 7 of 9

Hold the accelerometer pinned at a constant proper acceleration g forever. In Earth coordinates the velocity asymptotes to c — v/c = tanh(gτ/c) — and the worldline is not a parabola but a hyperbola: (x + c²/g)² − (ct)² = (c²/g)². Its asymptotes are light rays: behind the rocket there is a Rindler horizon at distance c²/g — about 0.97 light-years for 1g — beyond which light signals can never catch up, however long they chase.

Meanwhile γ = cosh(gτ/c) grows exponentially in the traveller’s own time: after 1 year at 1g the rocket does 0.77c (γ ≈ 1.58); after 2 years, 0.97c (γ ≈ 3.99); after 5 years γ ≈ 86. Earth time t = (c/g) sinh(gτ/c) races ahead of ship time τ ever faster — sustained acceleration is the most powerful time-dilation machine special relativity allows, which is why the “1g rocket” is a staple of interstellar thought experiments.

Figure 8 — Hyperbolic motion. Constant proper acceleration looks like a hyperbola, because γ must keep growing to keep the felt acceleration constant while coordinate acceleration γ−3a shrinks toward zero. Higher g hugs the asymptote sooner and drags the horizon closer.

8 · Quasi-3D: cones & spirals

Step 8 of 9

Everything so far flattened space to one dimension. Restore y and the light “cone” earns its name: at each instant, light spreads over a circle in the x–y plane whose radius grows at c, stacking into a genuine cone in (x, y, ct). Worldlines are now curves threading the cone’s interior. This view pays off immediately for circular motion: a particle in a steady orbit has constant speed — γ never changes — yet it accelerates continuously (centripetal acceleration), and its worldline is a helix winding around the time axis.

Figure 9 — The light cone in 2+1 dimensions. “Quasi-3D”: two space axes drawn in perspective, time vertical. The blue and teal lines across the top ellipse are the two diameters of the cone’s base circle — the x and y diameters, at right angles in the 3D space, each endpoint lying on the projected ellipse. The cone itself is rotationally symmetric, so it is this cross (and the curved worldline) that shows the rotation when you drag the viewing-angle slider in Step 8 — the same slider that rotates Figure 10. Every worldline you have seen is a slice of a picture like this.

The orbiting clock’s rate never varies: dτ/dt = 1/γ ≈ 0.64 the whole way round, even though its acceleration vector rotates continuously. Direction of acceleration is irrelevant to the rate — only the speed history enters the integral. Storage-ring muons live exactly this life (Step 9).

9 · What acceleration does not do

Step 9 of 9

Acceleration does…

Change the velocity, hence γ, moment by moment — it steers the worldline’s slope. Break symmetries: whoever accelerates is unambiguously the one who changed frames. Decide the total: through v(t), it fixes the value of the proper-time integral, so different acceleration programmes between the same events yield different elapsed times.

Acceleration does not…

Add its own term to the rate. There is no a in dτ = dt/γ. Need to be large to matter: a feather-touch turnaround still breaks the twin symmetry. Act like gravity in SR — the resemblance (the equivalence principle) is real but is general relativity’s story; in flat Minkowski spacetime, acceleration alone produces no extra “gravitational” dilation beyond the velocity integral.

The evidence

The bridge to gravity. Einstein’s equivalence principle says a uniformly accelerating rocket’s cabin is locally indistinguishable from standing in gravity — and in the rocket, the velocity-integral already makes the nose clock and tail clock tick at different steady rates (they have different velocity histories in any single inertial frame… and the cabin’s own Rindler coordinates make the rate difference look exactly like gravitational redshift, Δτ/τ ≈ gh/c²). Acceleration in flat spacetime is thus the training ground where gravitational time dilation is first visible — but the Minkowski formula above remains the complete SR account.

Lab · Design a twin trip

The Lab

No instantaneous turnarounds here. Your traveller accelerates at a steady, felt g up to cruising speed, coasts, decelerates to a stop at the star, then repeats the whole pattern home. All four burn phases are hyperbolic-motion segments from Step 7; the coasts are Step 3. The totals come from the Step 5 integral, evaluated exactly for this profile. If the star is too close for the chosen g and cruise speed, the ship never reaches cruise — it peaks mid-way and starts braking immediately.

At 1g the burns are long — over a year of ship time each — yet most of the age gap is still earned coasting at γ = 1.67. Try 5g: the totals barely move. That is the clock hypothesis in your hands: the path through velocity space matters, the violence of the steering hardly does.

The whole story in four lines

1 · Interval: c²dτ² = c²dt² − dx² — space motion is subtracted from time.
2 · Rate: dτ = dt/γ(v) — velocity alone sets the instantaneous tick rate.
3 · Total: τ = ∫ dt/γ(v(t)) — and acceleration writes the v(t) inside.
4 · Constant g: v/c = tanh(gτ/c), worldline a hyperbola, horizon at c²/g.

So: does acceleration cause time dilation? Not directly — no term, no extra effect, at any instant. But remove acceleration from the universe and no clock could ever change its velocity, turn around, orbit, or come home — every worldline would be straight, every twin the same age, and the integral would have nothing interesting to integrate. Acceleration is the author of every time-dilation story; velocity is just its handwriting.
